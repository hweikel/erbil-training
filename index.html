What is AI?

What is AI?

Explaining difficult concepts by level

  • To a young child: a computer that learns from examples instead of being given rules.
  • To a middle student: we show it thousands of examples, it finds the patterns, then it guesses about things it has never seen.
  • To an older student: a system trained on existing data to predict the next item in a sequence, which is why it can sound so convincing and still be wrong.

What is AI?

Rigorous definitions are hard

  • Nobody agrees what counts as improper use, or even what AI is
  • Major institutions in the US and UK are in public crisis over these questions
  • But the definitions and confidence have to come before rules or policies

What is AI?

What is AI literacy?

  • Understanding what these systems do, how they fail, and when not to use them
  • Not always the same as technical knowledge, but there are strong overlaps
  • Thesis of this course: “Mastery before amplification”

What is AI?

Why a curriculum is the strongest response

  • The response in the US and UK has largely been retreat: handwritten exams, no homework, surveillance during online exams
  • Our goal is the opposite: helping students understand these tools, use them better, and then decide for themselves what role they’ll play in their lives
  • Western schools got these tools earlier, but you are ahead by choosing to teach these tools deliberately

How these systems work

Not like other computer programs

Ordinary software

  • Written by a person
  • Rules you can read
  • Same input, same output
  • Errors can be fixed in the code

These systems

  • Patterns that emerged, not rules that were written
  • Rules invisible even to their makers
  • Same input, different output
  • Errors need retraining

How these systems work

How a model learns

  • Supervised learning: it is shown examples with the answers marked
  • Unsupervised learning: it finds patterns in data with no answers given
  • The data is bought, licensed, or scraped from the open internet
  • Modern models do all three in sequence: pre-training (unsupervised), fine-tuning (supervised), then alignment to human feedback (RLHF)

How these systems work

Predicting the next token

  • Language is broken into small chunks called tokens and turned into numbers
  • The model produces a probability distribution over what comes next
  • It is simply a mathematical operation, enabled by computers & energy

How these systems work

The model is not the tool

  • The model: finished learning, weights frozen
  • The context window: what it can see right now
  • The harness: what it is allowed to do — search, run code, use files
  • The interface: how you talk to it
  • When a tool improves, the model often has not changed at all

How these systems work

Modality and multimodality

  • A modality is one kind of information: text, image, sound, video
  • Multimodal means one system handles more than one at once
  • A class is a category a system sorts things into: cat / dog / neither

04

AI “failure modes”

AI failure modes

Non-determinism

  • The same input does not produce the same output
  • You cannot promise a student what the tool will say

AI failure modes

Sycophancy

  • Models are trained on human approval, so they learn to please their users
  • They will agree with you, flatter you, and support your worst ideas

AI failure modes

Bland answers

  • Statistical tools are bad at extremes
  • They “regress to the mean” of their data by design
  • Which is why AI-written work is so fluent and often annoying

AI failure modes

Modality blindspots

  • These are statistical representations of language, not human brains
  • Maths and spatial reasoning are structurally unreliable
  • Modern tools work around it by attaching older tools

AI failure modes

Biased training data

  • Scraping targets what is freely available online, which is mostly English
  • All data is incomplete, so all models are biased
  • Attempts to fix these problems produce more problems

Name 3 scientists. Fast!

AI failure modes

AI detectors don’t work

  • False positives are constant
  • Second-language students are accused most often
  • The alternatives: clear standards, and grading the process instead of the output

AI failure modes

Safety risks

  • Sycophancy and long conversations create psychological danger
  • Consumer chatbot protections do not work
  • Almost any mainstream tool can be broken with prompt injection

AI failure modes

“Failure modes” make great lessons

  • The model gives different answers → a lesson about non-determinism
  • The model is wrong → a lesson about checking sources
  • The model is biased → a lesson about training data

Protecting students

Rules alone do not work

  • The schools with the strictest policies are struggling most
  • The goal is student agency rather than a pre-defined relationship with AI
Problem

Cognitive debt

  • Using an LLM during writing changes brain activation, reducing neural connectivity and sense of ownership
  • Comparisons to calculators and search engines are not accurate
  • Too early to know the long-term effect on developing minds

Kosmyna et al. (2025), MIT Media Lab · arXiv:2506.08872 · brainonllm.com

Solution

Cognitive debt

  • Staged tool use
  • Mastery before amplification
Problem

The overcommitment trap

  • The climate around productivity, self-help, and ambition backs students into corners
  • Then AI misuse becomes the only way out
  • The most ambitious students tend to be hurt most in periods of early tool adoption
Solution

The overcommitment trap

  • Assess process, inputs, and understanding, not outputs
  • “Personal AI use constitution”
Problem

Discipline and fairness

  • Policies are vague because definitions are vague
  • Students get in trouble & distrust their teachers
  • Second-language students are accused disproportionately
Solution

Discipline and fairness

  • Make the policy specific enough to teach in 30 seconds
  • Enable success rather than punishing failure

Tools

How to pick a tool

  • What is the task? Choose the tool that fits it, not the most powerful one.
  • What happens to the lesson when it’s wrong or unavailable?
  • Be wary of tools that require accounts.

Tools

On access and cost

  • Ask your administration and IT department first
  • CONNECTED MENA may be able to find educator discounts or free credits
  • New tools are often free or cheap while they are growing
  • Don’t get attached to tools! They can triple in price or disappear overnight.

Chatbots

Chatbots

  • Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot — differences are minor
  • Do not get attached to one brand
  • Not always the right format — simply the one that’s commercially popular right now

Chatbots

Prompting

  • More: detail, structure, context
  • Give it “acceptance criteria”: rubrics, examples, constraints
  • Ask it to do one thing at a time

Chatbots

Grading with AI · risks

  • Disclose AI use to your students!
  • Give it the rubric and a marked example
  • Do not trust its scores on weaker work
  • Conversation history biases the marking. Use a separate project or account.
  • Narrative feedback is far more reliable than a score

Chatbots

Lesson plans with AI · risks

  • AI lesson plans are hard to tell apart from human ones, and score lower — more general, shallower, more rigid (Microsoft Research, Shiksha Copilot, 1,043 teachers)
  • Give it as many of your own materials as possible, then adapt by hand
  • Better: have it localise a lesson that already exists than write one from nothing

AI pedagogy

AI4K12 five big ideas

  • Perception: computers perceive the world using sensors.
  • Representation & Reasoning: agents maintain representations of the world and use them for reasoning.
  • Learning: computers can learn from data.
  • Natural Interaction: intelligent agents need many kinds of knowledge to interact naturally with humans.
  • Societal Impact: AI can impact society in both positive and negative ways.
  • Every lesson in this curriculum cites one of these

AI pedagogy

Brookings Institution framework, 2026

  • Prosper: use well-designed AI tools to genuinely improve teaching and learning.
  • Prepare: build the AI literacy educators, students, and families need to engage responsibly.
  • Protect: safeguard children’s development, wellbeing, and data from AI’s risks.

Answering hard questions

Round 1 — what the guide says

  • Tomato: for some cards there is no perfect answer. What matters is having a real reason — a tomato is a fruit because of how it grows, even though we cook it like a vegetable. Both facts are true, the rule just has to say which one counts. (G3-05)
  • True for most: it depends how you say it. “Many people who like X also like Y” is an honest pattern. “Everyone who likes X is Y” claims something about every single person, and that is usually not true. Name the real pattern with “some” or “many.” (G6-08)

Answering hard questions

Round 2 — what would you say?

  • “It gave a source, so it is checked.” (Grade 9)
  • “The AI is my source.” (Grade 9)
  • “Finding the AI wrong means I failed.” (Grade 9)
  • “AI can do it, so we should.” (Grade 10)
  • “One side of the debate is obviously right.” (Grade 10)

Answering hard questions

Round 2 — what the guide says

  • A named source is not a read one — open it. The AI can misattribute a real source or invent one entirely. (G9-10)
  • The AI is never the citation. You cite the reliable source you opened and verified. (G9-10)
  • Catching the AI being wrong is the skill, not a failure. A found error is a success. (G9-10)
  • Can is not should. Capability is not a reason. (G10-18)
  • If one side is obviously right, it is not a real tradeoff — both sides have to be genuine goods. (G10-22)

Questions from yesterday

How will students be assessed?

  • Every lesson carries its own assessment.
  • K–6: a short class-level check the teacher fills in after the lesson — no student scores, no student accounts.
  • Grades 7–12: a four-level rubric per student, plus a self-assessment prompt and a peer review prompt.
  • Grades 4–12 also sit a summative assessment twice a year.
  • Nothing rewards memorisation. Everything looks at skill, reasoning, depth of work and participation.

Questions from yesterday

How do we get students to work on projects when there’s so much else going on in harder classes?

  • All project work happens in class, so it does not compete with other subjects.
  • Projects are highly structured — one lesson, one step, one artifact. There is little room to procrastinate.

Questions from yesterday

How do we manage our classrooms and time in interactive activities?

  • Structure.
  • Assign roles, make groups, move desks, set a timer.
  • Run several short rounds of a game with small changes, not one long one.
  • Keep it short. Never let the whole lesson become one big activity.

Questions from yesterday

Modality and multimodality

  • A modality is one kind of information: text, image, sound, video.
  • Multimodal means one system handles more than one kind at the same time.
  • A class (or category) is a group a system sorts things into: cat / dog / neither.
  • Classification is putting an example into the right class.
  • The word “class” in AI means a category, not a group of students.

Questions from yesterday

How do we encourage students to use AI ethically?

  • The lessons do this directly. Every lesson builds a mindset, a strategy or an instinct for good use.
  • Most lessons teach scepticism of the tool, not enthusiasm for it.
  • Students will use AI at home whatever we do. The goal is to prepare and protect them — not to speed up or encourage use.

Questions from yesterday

How do we get more tokens or access to free tools?

  • Ask your administration and your IT department.
  • CONNECTED MENA may be able to find educator discounts or free credits from companies.
  • Watch for new startups offering similar tools — they are often cheap or free while they grow.
  • Do not build a lesson around today’s price. These tools can double in cost at any time.

Questions from yesterday

Nuance

  • A small difference in meaning that changes the right answer.
  • “Most students” and “all students” look similar. They are not.
  • AI outputs often flatten nuance: they give the average answer, confidently.
  • Noticing the small difference is the human job. That is what we are training students to do.

Assessment intro

How assessment works in this curriculum

  • Process and reasoning matter more than the outcome.
  • A model that scores badly but was tested and reported honestly is stronger work than an inflated one.

Assessment intro

Three types of assessment

  • Self-assessment: the student states their own understanding in one honest sentence.
  • Peer review: a student checks one specific piece of a partner’s work against the same criteria.
  • Teacher record: a rubric level logged per criterion, building a picture of growth across the year.

Assessment intro

Where this gets hard

  • Students score their friends generously, and strangers harshly.
  • Feedback stays vague: “good job,” “nice work.”
  • Students who do not understand the criteria cannot check a partner against them.
  • Self-assessment turns into either bragging or apologising.
  • The reading level of the rubric is above the student.
  • It takes class time, and it feels like time not spent teaching.

Assessment intro

Discussion: How do you help students succeed at self-assessment and peer review?

Assessment intro

Practical strategies

  • Focus on the rubric — the criteria, not the person.
  • Give lots of structure. Ask for one specific check, not a general opinion.
  • Rotate pairs and groups regularly.
  • Keep these scores separate from class grades.
